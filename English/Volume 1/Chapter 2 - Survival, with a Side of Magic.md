People can survive as long as they have clothing, food, and shelter. In other words: clothes, food, and a house.

Losing the electricity didn't make my clothes or my house disappear, so that part was fine.

Food was the problem.

I'd known some kind of disaster was going on, but I'd figured the online grocery deliveries would start back up eventually. Without electricity, though, deliveries stopped too. Lazing around at home wasn't going to restock my food.

"Starving to death," something I'd never once thought about in my whole life, was suddenly a very real problem, so I panicked and took up hunting and gathering to put food on the table: fishing, trapping, and picking wild mountain vegetables.

I pushed into the mountains with a field guide to wild plants and mushrooms in hand.

I caught rabbits and raccoon dogs in traps I'd built using what I'd learned from anime and books in the community center library.

I dug a fishing rod out of the shed, baited it with earthworms and grasshoppers, and went fishing.

I was a total survival newbie, and my skills were pretty pathetic. Hunting alone couldn't cover the calories I burned each day, so I made up the difference by dipping into my food stockpile, which was already looking pretty thin.

My backyard vegetable patch, which I'd only kept as a hobby so I could eat fresh and healthy, became my lifeline, and I rushed to expand it.

Before the Crystal Disaster, when wild animals ate the vegetables in my backyard, all I did was sigh, "Ugh, what a pain." Now it made me murderous. Maybe I'll just kill the things and eat them.

But no matter what I tried, I couldn't grow or catch enough to cover my daily meals.

My rice and canned food dwindled bit by bit, and life was hard with no end in sight. Through all of it, my one comfort and my one hope was, of course, my magic wand Okutameteorite.

Today, once again, Okutameteorite had gotten me venison. With the deer on my shoulders, every step down the mountain was physically heavy, but my heart felt like it had sprouted wings.

My cute, cute magic wand-chan, my most treasured possession, worked as a medium for casting magic: if I sang at its natural frequency, it would fire a white beam.

The beam was about as thick as an elephant's leg—pretty damn thick—and it shot straight out along the line running from my mouth through Okutameteorite.

One shot hit hard enough to rock a small car.

I'd made the magic wand Okutameteorite as a hobby and brought it along for self-defense, but it turned out to be incredibly useful for hunting.

I didn't have a gun, and I obviously couldn't use a bow. I didn't have the arm strength to throw a spear, either.

So if I stumbled on a deer twenty or thirty meters off in the mountains, munching grass and watching me, I had no way to bring it down.

Not without a magic wand, anyway.

I took careful aim and hit the deer dead-on with a magic-wand beam. It didn't die instantly, but it couldn't move, so I just walked up and finished it off with a knife. Seriously, thank god for Okutameteorite.

I bled and gutted it on the spot to lighten the load, but a whole deer was still seriously heavy. By the time I got down the mountain and home, my legs were jelly and my shoulders and arms felt ready to burst. My whole body was screaming, but in a good way.

I set the deer down on the veranda and grinned, picturing how many dozens of kilos of meat it would give me.

That was way too much to eat in one go. It was only early spring, but with the fridge dead, it would rot if I left it out at room temperature. I'd have to slice it thin and sun-dry it.

Operation Sun-Dry had already worked on the pale chub I'd caught in the Tama River. If it worked for fish, it should work for meat too...

"...Huh?"

I'd been wondering whether I had enough room to hang all that meat when I noticed the fish I'd hung to dry from the veranda eaves was gone.

Weird. It had definitely been there when I left that morning. Figuring the wind might've blown it off, I searched all around the veranda, but it was nowhere to be found.

What I found instead was a set of faint shoeprints in the dirt.

A th-thief!? I screwed up!

Gripping Okutameteorite tight, I braced myself and started searching again, this time for a person instead of dried fish.

I'd been careless. Until that very moment, it had never even occurred to me that something like this could happen.

If civilization collapsed and production and distribution stopped, the food sitting in convenience stores and supermarkets would disappear in no time.

In a collapsed society, stealing food when you were short was actually the sensible thing to do.

When society had collapsed and nobody knew if they'd make it through tomorrow, the weird one was probably me, for casually taking up fishing, trapping, and farming.

Pissed at the rotten thief who'd swiped my precious food, I spent a good while combing every inch around the house, but I couldn't find them. The culprit was long gone.

Okutama had seemed completely empty of people, but apparently someone besides me was still around. Then again, if I'd stayed, it only made sense that others might have too.

At a time like this, I'd normally have called 110 for the police, but that was useless now. I had no choice but to take the loss.

Looking on the bright side, I was probably still doing all right on my own. I didn't have to loot. I was tilling my fields and catching deer to keep myself fed.

Sure, I was making up the shortfall from my stockpile, so it couldn't last forever. But I hadn't sunk so low that I'd steal someone else's food.

I'm not letting this ruined world beat me. I'm doing great. Admirable, even.

Telling myself that over and over wasn't going to bring back the lost food, but at least it took the edge off my anger and panic.

From now on, I'll be careful not to let anyone take my food...

Two months into survival mode, my daily routine had settled into a rhythm.

I used to be a hardcore night owl, but fuel for lights was too precious to waste now, so I kept to daylight hours.

Every morning I got up, drew water from the well, ate something I'd cooked ahead of time, and headed into the mountains to check my traps, with Okutameteorite along for self-defense.

I picked wild mountain vegetables on the way back, then took my fishing rod down to the river.

After lunch came odd jobs: sharpening my knife, butchering and smoking whatever meat and fish I'd caught, fixing roof tiles the crystal rain had cracked, that sort of thing.

After dinner, I played at target shooting with my magic wand or read a book, and then it was bedtime.

Firewood was too precious to waste, so I only took a bath every two or three days.

Judging from the notices and messages posted on the doors of houses and general stores, most people in Okutama had left for the shelters in neighboring Ome City, either because supplies had run short or to seek rescue. Wherever I walked, I never ran into a soul.

Several houses had windows that had clearly been smashed in, and some cars had their fuel doors hanging open. No doubt about it: while I'd been lazing around without a care in the middle of an unprecedented disaster, looters had come through.

So I'd been living a sort of hunting-gathering-farming hybrid life, keeping an eye out for any looters still lurking around, but today things were a little different.

A really bizarre rabbit had turned up in one of my mountain traps.

At first glance, it looked like an ordinary wild rabbit.

But there was a red gem embedded in its forehead.

A tumor? A parasite? No. That's not it.

The thing was about the size of my pinkie nail, and when I touched it, it felt hard, like stone.

It was a mineral.

The words "magical life-form" flashed through my head.

If there are magic stones and magic, wouldn't there be magical life-forms too...?

This world has gotten pretty fantasy-like in just three months.

I carefully carried the dead, deformed rabbit home and laid it on the workbench in my workroom for a closer look.

The first thing I learned was that the rabbit itself was basically just a normal rabbit.

I wasn't a vet, so I couldn't claim to know rabbit anatomy all that well, but after nearly two months of this, I'd gotten pretty used to butchering animals. At the very least, I could tell it didn't have anything freaky going on—two hearts, no brain, mystery organs, that kind of thing. It looked safe to eat.

I set the dissected rabbit aside for now and moved on to the pinkie-nail-sized gem from its forehead.

The gem was a slightly lopsided sphere, ruby red.

It wasn't unheard of for living things to produce minerals or mineral-like things.

Akoya pearl oysters made pearls, and ivory was prized as a material for art. Tortoiseshell, made from the shell of a kind of sea turtle, was prettier than plenty of gems and fetched a high price.

But I'd never heard of rabbits producing gems like this.

After a moment's thought, I tried scratching the red gem with a chip of industrial diamond.

Not a scratch.

M-Mohs hardness of 11!

Wait, is this a magic stone too!?

I took it out to the backyard and sang the natural-frequency note at it, and a thin white beam came sputtering limply out. It was nothing next to Okutameteorite's, but it was a beam.

It's a freaking magic stone! So there's more than one!

My heart leaped at my first new discovery in ages.

I'd thought only Okutameteorite, which I'd carved out of a meteorite, was special.

But I'd been wrong.

And if that was the case, then...

I gathered up some of the electric crystals that had come down with the heavy rain and were still lying by the roadside, and sang the same natural-frequency note at those bead-sized crystals, but nothing happened: no beam, no resonance.

Well, yeah, obviously. If electric crystals fired beams too, all the random ones scattered on the ground would've been spraying beams everywhere whenever I played with my magic wand.

An object's natural frequency changes with its size and shape even when the material's the same, so it made sense that the note that resonated with Okutameteorite wouldn't make a different gem resonate.

But what if the resonance wasn't what mattered, and it was a particular sound that did? What if Okutameteorite's natural frequency had just happened to match that sound?

I clamped one of the tiny electric crystals from the crystal rain in place with my tools.

Slowly and gently, so I wouldn't crack the lopsided little thing, I ground it down into a ball. The cutting was as delicate as painting a picture on a grain of rice. Once that was done, I went by the feel of my fingertips and polished it with abrasive into a perfect sphere.

The finished sphere was so tiny I'd never find it again if I dropped it. When I sang the natural-frequency note at it, a feeble white beam dribbled out, like it had wet itself.

It actually fires a beam! So you were a magic stone too!?

Well, I guess that figures? Does it?

Now that I knew the result, it did seem reasonable enough.

Okutameteorite fell into my backyard on the same day electric crystals spread across the world. I'd have been crazy to think the two weren't connected.

My otaku soul caught fire.

Electric crystals are everywhere. Magic-wand materials are everywhere.

I can mass-produce magic wands with this.

Amazing. This is amazing!

Wahahahahaha!

I'm a world-class Wand Maker!

I'm gonna make them! I'm gonna make magic wands! Tons and tons of them!

...Not that I had anywhere to sell them.

Man, if only online auctions were still around. I could sell real magic wands and make a killing, and feed my pride as a craftsman while I was at it. It would've been perfect.

I was dancing around in delight, with only a twinge of regret, when the clatter of my alarm clappers came from the veranda and dumped cold water all over my excitement.

My heart jumped, and I froze.

When I strained my ears, I could tell someone was definitely out there.

Fell right into my alarm trap, didn't you, looter! This time, I'm ready for you!

I grabbed an iron pipe, crept down the hallway, and peeked out through a gap in the storm shutters.

If there was a whole group of them, I couldn't win, and I'd just have to take the loss again. But if it was one person, I'd beat them up and send them running.

I'll do it. I'll really do it...!

When I scanned the veranda by the faint moonlight, I saw only one person there.

Just one child.

The girl stretched up on tiptoe with all her might to take down the mesh basket of raisins I'd hung to dry high under the eaves, and now she was stuffing them into a battered school backpack, glancing nervously around the whole time.

It was too dark to make out her face, but her arms were as thin as dead twigs, and she was scrawny even for a kid.

She looked so small and frail that one swing of an iron pipe would snap her in two.

I was stunned.

This obviously feeble little brat is who's been stealing my food? And I was scared of her?

Damn brat. Messing with a grown-up like that...!

I hurried back to the living room, stuffed a plastic bag with my homemade pickled daikon, and tied up a bundle of salty dried meat and smoked fish with string.

Then I went straight back to where the little shit was committing her crime, yanked the gap in the shutters wide open for a split second, flung the food at her, and slammed them shut.

"Ah!?"

"..."

The girl let out a startled cry, but I ignored it.

Hmph. Damn brat!

Don't mess with grown-ups!

A sensible adult can spare food for a child with a future ahead of them, even in a crisis.

How about that, huh? Bet a little shit who can only stay alive by looting could never pull that off.

I was busy silently congratulating myself on my extraordinary social skills when I heard the patter of little feet, and then a timid child's voice from right on the other side of the shutter.

"Um, thank you..."

"Whoa! J-Just take that and go! Get lost! Don't talk to me! Shoo!"

My first conversation with another human being in months was murder on my heart. I screamed for dear life as my legs gave out and I scrambled backward, and luckily, the terrifying brat who'd attacked me with the weapon known as conversation went away.

Damn it, this is why kids are such a pain. Didn't your mom ever teach you not to talk to strangers!? Don't talk to me! My stomach can't take it!

I'd given the backpack girl plenty of food, so I figured she'd be satisfied and move on. The exact opposite happened.

Every few days, the brat snuck over in the dead of night and made off with the food hanging under my eaves.

In exchange, she left behind little odds and ends she'd apparently scavenged somewhere else: sewing scissors, a hand mirror, hairpins, sunscreen.

I guess they were supposed to be thank-you gifts.

But I didn't need any of that. Her picks were way too girly, and they were no use to me, practical or hobby-wise.

For a while, I let her take the food without a word, but my supplies were shrinking even with just me to feed, and I couldn't keep handing out charity forever.

Then again, she was so skinny she was headed straight for starvation if I didn't share, and I'd have felt guilty just abandoning her. If I stepped out one morning and found her lying starved to death on my doorstep, I'd never sleep easy again.

So I gave the girl a self-sufficiency kit and asked her to kindly move out.

She was probably elementary-school age, and the only ways she knew to feed herself were stealing and begging. So I put together a package for her: a little booklet that explained in plain terms how to identify wild mountain vegetables and grow crops, a kid-sized fishing rod I'd carved by hand from good wood, vegetable seeds, clean bandages, a small bottle of disinfectant, and more.

Along with a note that said, "Get lost and never come back."

I didn't hand it over in person, just left it on the veranda, so I never saw how she reacted.

But the kit was gone from the veranda the next morning, and after that, the noises in the night stopped.

The little looter who'd been quietly threatening my way of life was gone. I was sure she'd do just fine somewhere far away from my house.

And if she couldn't make it even after all the setup I'd given her, she'd just die. Of course, if I couldn't make it from here on out, I'd probably starve too, so let's call it even.

For a whole year after the Crystal Disaster, I did my best to survive in my own way.

If I'd clung to some Buddha-like urge to feed a brat whose name and face I didn't even know, a mercy way beyond my means, we'd both have gone down early, from the stress of dealing with people or from hunger. I was no Buddha, and in a merciless world like this, showing mercy to others would just get me killed.

I figured giving her the means to fend for herself and then doing catch-and-release was about as rational as a civilized person could get in this ruined world.

Nature bared its fangs without mercy at us civilized folk, helpless now that civilization was gone.

Spring through fall wasn't so bad. I could fish, pick vegetables from my field, and catch animals in my traps.

Winter was the beginning of the end. Fish stopped taking the bait, I couldn't get vegetables anymore, and animals stopped showing themselves.

My firewood and food stockpile got eaten away fast, and I learned the hard way just how helpless humans were without civilization.

When I bumped into a Bear-san who'd flunked hibernation, I straight-up peed myself. I managed to drive it off by nailing it with five or six beams, which I owed entirely to my daily target practice. That was way too close. Without Okutameteorite, I'd have been dead.

A year of being suddenly forced to survive had improved my survival skills by leaps and bounds.

I got better at hiding traps and setting them so they wouldn't misfire.

I learned the right moment to set the hook when I got a bite, and I reinforced the fence around my garden to keep pests from wrecking it.

But when spring finally came after that brutal winter, I found myself staring helplessly at a bone-dry rice bin, a heap of empty cans, and a salt jar without a grain left in it.

Survival is way too hard. How did people in prehistoric times manage to live on hunting and gathering alone? I seriously respect them.

My daily routine had stabilized, and I had a way to drive off danger, but there was nothing I could do about the food shortage.

I'd dug up an abandoned rice paddy nearby and gotten it back into working shape, so come fall, a few months from now, I'd have rice to harvest.

Sure, I was an amateur, but I'd played a rice-farming game before and read a rice-growing handbook I'd found at the agricultural cooperative, so I could expect a decent harvest.

That still didn't fix my immediate food problem, though.

I had a few months until the rice harvest, and somehow I had to keep myself fed until then.

Fishing, trapping, and hunting with my magic wand just weren't enough, no matter what I did.

Magic wands were useful, but they couldn't solve everything.

My trusty partner, Okutameteorite-chan, was now enshrined as the guardian deity of my home, and these days I carried a mass-produced magic wand instead.

This magic wand, Hendensho-kun, was pretty capable. I'd carved it with a new processing method, using electric crystals I'd collected on a trip out to Okutama Substation over the winter.

I got the idea for the method from the red-gem rabbits.

The Crystal Disaster seemed to have affected wildlife too. After that first red-gem rabbit, I kept running into animals with gems in their foreheads, bellies, chests, forelegs, and so on.

They were downright "magical."

There were deer that let out a cry, wrapped themselves in lightning, and bolted at super speed, and raccoon dogs that cried out, froze me in place with a glare, and ran off while I was stuck. Some of their tricks were seriously startling.

They always cried out when they used magic, so I figured those cries activated the magic in their gems, the same way I bellowed in my stupidly loud voice to use mine.

Well, I didn't really know the details. I'd never once managed to take down an animal that used magic to escape, so I hadn't been able to observe one up close or catch one to study. The only sample I had was the red-gem rabbit.

When I examined its red gem closely, I found it had a double-layered structure: a gemstone core wrapped in an outer layer of gemstone.

Once I'd polished the rabbit gemstone into a perfect sphere, it surprised me by putting out a far stronger beam than its size should have allowed.

My data so far said beam power was proportional to the size of the magic stone: the bigger the stone, the stronger and thicker the beam.

Yet the pinkie-nail-sized red gem fired a beam that, by my calculations, matched what a thumbnail-sized stone should put out.

I suspected the secret to that high output lay in the double-layered structure, so I set out to prove it with an experiment.

First, I carved a fairly large electric crystal into a sphere and split it in half. Then I hollowed out the inside to make a spherical cavity and fitted in a smaller electric-crystal sphere, carved to fit perfectly. All that was left was to glue the shell back together.

In theory, that should have boosted the power. It didn't. All I got was the output of the electric crystal I'd used for the core.

Apparently, a magic stone lost its magical properties once it was broken. My plan to piece tiny fragments together into one giant crystal had flopped too, so this approach was probably a dead end.

So I went the ship-in-a-bottle route.

The idea was to drill a small hole in a crystal sphere, slip a hook in through it, and carve away the inside, cutting a sphere within the sphere without ever breaking it open.

An ordinary person probably couldn't pull off work that delicate.

But I was seriously good with my hands. I wouldn't call it a piece of cake, but without too much trouble, I carved a slightly smaller sphere inside a marble-sized one.

Then I filled the gap between the inner and outer spheres with resin, and it worked: I'd reproduced the rabbit crystal's boost in beam power.

As a bonus, the little hole I'd drilled to carve out the inside became a firing port for the beam, which improved its directionality.

And that was how I completed the general-purpose, mass-produced magic wand Hendensho.

Armed with Hendensho, I was about to head down the mountain and into town.

Then I'd come back with food, medical supplies, and whatever else I could recover!

Until now, I'd refused to steal or loot, telling myself that wasn't something human beings did.

Part of it, too, was simply that I was scared of bumping into other looters, and I'd kept making excuses so I could go on living alone.

But I couldn't afford to be picky anymore.

I couldn't survive anymore unless I took from others.

Okutama had already run dry, so I needed to get out of Okutama and look elsewhere.

I was done with my stupid pride.

I had no idea what things were like in the city center now.

Maybe it was all ruins. Or maybe, surprisingly, there was a survivor community still going strong and living a stable life.

Either way, all I could do was pray I wouldn't run into anyone on my scavenging-and-looting run.
