I harvested the rice, threshed it, hulled it, packed the finished white rice into handmade straw bales, and stacked them in the shed. With that, my preparations for this winter were done.

Last year, once the cold set in, the fish stopped biting and animals stopped wandering into my traps, and I had to nervously eat into what little I had stored. This year was different.

I had plenty of rice. The Blue Witch was sending canned food and dried noodles my way too.

I could eat my fill every day without worrying about running out. What greater happiness could there be?

Good thing I'd taken up rice farming. Good thing I'd finished the fertility-magic bypass incantation. I was grateful for everything.

Still, there was room for improvement. Fertility magic had doubled my rice harvest, sure, but all it did was double it. The quality hadn't gone up.

The grains from my first-ever crop were a little skinny, slimmer and smaller than the rice sold before civilization collapsed. I was growing a standard variety, so the problem was probably how I'd grown it.

Of course a first-year rice farmer couldn't grow top-quality rice right off the bat, but it still stung a little.

Also, getting kidnapped by a dragon had thrown everything into chaos and pushed back the harvest, so the sparrows had eaten a good chunk of it. I was about ready to hate anything with wings. Unforgivable.

But the rice didn't taste bad, and I'd planted extra in case of a poor harvest, so even after all my mistakes, I'd harvested more than enough to get me through the next year.

I'd caught and dried fish, smoked venison, and had the Blue Witch pick up sugar and whale oil for me at the barter market. So this year, I could sit back at my window and watch the snow dust the mountains white. It was a world away from last year's desperate early winter.

I had so much breathing room that I decided to invite the Blue Witch over. This year I didn't need to trek into the mountains hunting for scraps of food, or force myself to sleep extra hours to save energy. I could actually do whatever I wanted and have fun! That was how much breathing room I had.

“Hello, Blue Witch? Wanna come by my place right now? Let's play a card game!”

I made the call through an eyeball familiar, and after a few beats, the Blue Witch answered in a sleepy voice.

“...What? What about cards? At this hour...? The sun's barely up.”

“A card game. I'll lend you a deck and teach you the rules. I told you about it before.”

“Hmm, a card game... You mean the kind where you summon girls and gems and stuff? My little sister played that, so I know a bit about it.”

“That's Gemma. I play Gath.”

“Huh...? What's the difference?”

The Blue Witch knew next to nothing about card games, and her responses were pretty halfhearted, but I kept going anyway, walking her through the history of trading card games and all the different kinds in painstaking detail. Partway through, she cut me off, sounding exasperated.

“Okay, okay. I get it loud and clear: you really love card games. Fine, I'll play along. Do I come over now?”

“Oh! Hell yeah. You haven't had breakfast yet, right? I'll make something, and we can play while we eat. Man, this'll be the first time in my life I've hung out with a friend. That's supposed to be fun, right? Time to find out if the rumors are true.”

“Don't say sad stuff like that... I'll be there in thirty minutes.”

With that, the Blue Witch hung up.

Was there anything sad about what I'd just said? She was a hard one to figure out.

I made three pickled-plum rice balls, set out some pickled daikon and brown-rice tea to go with them, and was putting charcoal in the kotatsu[^1] when the Blue Witch showed up with snow in her hair.

It must have been cold out there, but her black coat hung open in front, and her thighs were completely bare. Yikes, I'm getting cold just looking at her.

“Hey. Aren't you cold in that?”

“Hm? Not really. Honestly, I'm more comfortable when it's this cold. I've liked the cold ever since I became a witch.”

“Huh. What about heat?”

“Same as anyone. Oh, you got the kotatsu out? Can I get in?”

I nodded, and the Blue Witch shoved her legs under the kotatsu and let out a breath. Liking the cold apparently didn't stop her from liking a kotatsu.

The snow fell thick and steady, as if it were swallowing every sound, and time slipped quietly by while I spread cards across the kotatsu and gave her a beginner's course.

By noon, the Blue Witch had gotten the hang of the fairly complicated rules and could play real matches with a simple deck.

Now came the fun part. When I pulled out a new deck to teach her special combos and technical plays, the Blue Witch slumped, tossed her cards aside, and put both hands up.

“Wait, wait. How much are you trying to cram in one day? Give me a break. My head's going to explode.”

“Oh, fair point? Sorry. I was having fun. I figured it'd be about the same as playing both sides by myself, but I guess card games really are meant for two.”

“Obviously. I've got the rules down, more or less, but I don't really see the appeal of playing at magic and monsters and life points with cards when there's real magic and real monsters around.”

“What? You just don't get it. See, it's because... Huh. Why is it, actually?”

We both tilted our heads.

Fantasy, once pure make-believe, had become real along with the catastrophe. That changed where fantasy-driven games, manga, and card games stood too.

Then again, chefs probably read cooking manga too. Having the real thing around doesn't stop people from loving the made-up version.

“Is there anything that got more fun after the world changed? Besides making magic wands.”

“That's just you. Hmm.”

The Blue Witch gathered up the cards scattered over the table and put them away, thinking it over for a while. After a bit, something seemed to occur to her.

“Monsters, maybe. They're a pain, but some of them are cute.”

“Ahh.”

I pictured the lovely stoat professor (beast form) and nodded deeply. True.

Professor Ohinata wasn't a monster, but she was, without question, a blessing the Gremlin Disaster had brought the world: cuteness incarnate. Wasn't her turning back into a human a loss for all humanity?

“Cute monsters are fun just to watch. If they're cute and harmless, sometimes I just chase them off.”

“And cute ones that are harmful?”

“I kill them.”

The Blue Witch's answer was short and merciless. Well, yeah. You throw killing intent around even at people, so of course you would. Sca-ry.

We broke for lunch, then decided to spend the afternoon walking around looking for monsters.

We'd spent all morning on the game I'd picked and given our brains a workout, so the afternoon would go to her pick: a walk to get our bodies moving.

I bundled up in heavy winter gear and pulled on my winter boots in the entryway. The Blue Witch, meanwhile, stayed as light as ever, sticking with that half-assed outfit of hers that was too hot in summer and too cold in winter.

I waved my spare pair of fluffy earmuffs at her, offering to lend them, but she held up a palm and flatly turned them down. You really don't mind the cold, huh?

“All right. I'm ready. Let's go!”

“Yeah. There's a cute monster you can only see in winter. Let's look for it while we walk around Okutama.”

“Okay!”

We headed out together into a silver world under a thin layer of snow.

We ambled along the snowy road by the Tama River, but there were fewer creatures around than I'd expected. It was winter, so that was only natural, but maybe because I had a stupidly strong creature walking right next to me, the animals seemed even scarcer than usual.

My breath trailed away white, while the Blue Witch's had no color at all. Little things like that reminded me that even though she looked human, she wasn't human after all.

After we'd walked for a while, the Blue Witch murmured.

“...It really is peaceful around here. It's all weak monsters. Nothing comes at me when it sees me.”

“Yeah, pretty much. No clue if it's the area or what, but the only things that show up are weak enough for Hendensho to chase off.”

I waved my <ruby>Hendensho<rt>magic wand</rt></ruby> to show her. The Blue Witch kept ambling along and tapped a deciduous branch bowed under the snow with the tip of Cyanos, knocking a clump of snow down onto the road.

“That's a good thing. Better if there aren't any strong monsters.”

“But strong monsters give good materials, right? It'd be a problem if they weren't around.”

Monsters ran the whole range, from pathetic to terrifying.

At one end were super-weak monsters that were literally just slugs with hair on them, things that died if you so much as stepped on them. At the other end were giant-kaiju-class disaster creatures that would still cause casualties even if a whole pack of ordinary Transcendents took them on together.

The Gremlins weak monsters had were small and poor quality.

The Gremlins strong monsters had were big and high quality.

And to harvest the large Gremlins that made excellent core materials for wands, it was better to have strong monsters around.

I gave her my take as a Wand Maker, and the Blue Witch smashed the snowball she'd been packing right into my face.

“Bwaa!?”

“Idiot. Strong monsters usually attack people. If you could get high-end leather out of them, would you let a pack of man-eating tigers prowl around your house?”

“W-Well, that'd be kind of...”

“Exactly. It's true you can get good materials from strong monsters, but it isn't worth it.”

According to the Blue Witch, who had plenty of experience hunting monsters, putting monster materials to use came with a lot of problems.

Those problems fell into three broad categories.

First, monsters were hard to kill.

The monsters I'd fought life-and-death battles against in Okutama, Hendensho in hand, were small fry as far as strength went. Central Tokyo was crawling with monsters many times stronger than the ones I'd struggled to drive off.

Man-eaters popped up in the city as regularly as a bus schedule, so people died as a matter of course. Right now, only witches and mages could take down monsters above a certain strength.

Second, monster meat was inedible.

Monsters came in all kinds—chimera types, beast types, plant types, ghost types—but none of them were edible. The meat didn't taste bad, and some of it was actually delicious, but it wrecked your stomach.

I knew that firsthand, since I'd tried everything to get it down and thrown up more times than I could count. Boil it or grill it, you still couldn't eat monster meat.

And on top of that, monsters ravaged fields and crops just like boars and deer did, which was a real headache.

Say you finally killed some hateful monster that had gotten fat gorging itself on the vegetables you'd lovingly raised. You'd want to at least eat it and win back those calories, but no such luck. Those monsters really pissed me off.

The exception was witches and mages, who could eat monster meat just fine.

Monsters ate each other all the time, survival-of-the-fittest style, and seemed fine, so Transcendents, who had mutated from humans into something inhuman, were probably physically closer to monsters.

The last problem was deterioration.

Monster materials were better than ordinary animal materials.

Their claws were sharp, their hides were tough, and their bones were sturdy.

You could even get materials with bizarre, magical properties you'd never believe from looking at them.

But high-performance materials like that deteriorated over time, and magic power was to blame.

Monsters were magical by nature, so they carried magic power.

That magic power stayed in the body even after death, which was what let monster materials perform like magic.

But flip that around, and it meant that once the magic power was gone, so was the performance.

Every living thing gradually lost its magic power after death, and as the magic power drained away, the performance deteriorated with it.

It depended on the material, but somewhere between a few days and a month at most, it lost all its magic power and was completely spent.

Bones as strong as steel turned into ordinary bones, and dazzling feathers lost their shine and became plain old feathers.

Even if you risked your life to kill a monster, you couldn't eat its meat, and the rest of the materials soon deteriorated into junk. It wasn't worth it at all.

I got sick of listening to the Blue Witch rattle off “Everything Wrong with Monster Materials!”

They're pests while they're alive, and killing them gets you nothing. Monsters are a total lost cause, man.

“So there's no upside to killing monsters at all...”

“Right. But Ori's changed that a little.”

“Me?”

Confused, I pointed at myself, and the Blue Witch gave a little smile.

“Out of all the materials you can get from monsters, only Gremlins don't deteriorate with time. They were too hard to process to be any use, but you, Ori, can process them. Only you can. You can draw out their performance and turn them into magic wands.”

“Oh! No, yeah, you're right. That's exactly it. That giant frozen kaiju you killed—once it's done thawing, we can probably pull a fucking huge Gremlin out of it. Let's take down strong monsters left and right and rake in big Gremlins! We'll make magic wands like crazy!”

“And we're the ones who'd be risking our lives fighting monsters for that.”

“...Sorry.”

In the end, it always came back to that.

Monsters had things to offer, but they just weren't worth the risk.

That was the whole point.

For the sake of the people putting their lives on the line against monsters, I need to make even higher-performance magic wands.

Make better wands.

Have them use those to defeat stronger monsters.

Get higher-quality Gremlins.

Use those Gremlins to make even better wands.

It'd be great if we could get a cycle like that going.

We'd covered a lot of ground while we talked, and Lake Okutama came into view. The mountains and forest that had closed in around us fell away, the view opened up wide, and thick clouds sat on the far side of the sky. If we didn't turn back soon, we'd probably get caught in heavy snow.

We still hadn't found the cute monster the Blue Witch was after.

“So what kind of monster are we looking for, anyway?”

“It's a fluffy white wolf-type thing you only see when there's snow on the ground. It has a blue Gremlin on its forehead. It's probably a pet dog or something that mutated. It's pretty docile for a monster and doesn't attack people.”

“Huh.”

“I want to pet it, but it runs if I get too close. So I just watch.”

The Blue Witch opened and closed both hands longingly and sighed.

She's really into animals. I get liking stoats, but are wolves actually cute...? I'd call them cool, if anything.

We walked along the shore of Lake Okutama for a while, but there was no sign of the white wolf. The most we found was a turtle monster with an absurdly long neck, swimming in the lake as the cold wind whipped up little waves. Several small turtles were perched on its huge shell, a good 2 m across, looking this way and that, which was kind of funny. A parent and its kids, maybe?

I wish monsters would stop reminding me that they aren't unknowable horrors but living creatures doing their best to get by day to day. It just makes them harder to kill, you know?

As we walked, chatting now and then, the sun sank lower and the air got colder. The falling snowflakes grew bigger and heavier, and it started to feel like time to call it a day.

“Hey, let's head back soon. We can try again tomorrow.”

“Mm... A little longer. I feel like we'll find it if we look just a little longer.”

“Really? Ah!?”

I'd been staring blankly at the strip where the road met the mountainside, wondering whether to just leave the Blue Witch and head home alone, when I spotted something incredible and couldn't help yelling.

Footprints.

Footprints, pressed into the fresh snow.

They were shaped just like the prints of an ordinary bare human foot, but by my eye they were a whopping 40 cm long.

“Wh-What!? Bigfoot!? It actually exists!”

“What is it? What did you find?”

A huge discovery from a totally unexpected angle sent adrenaline racing through my brain. I knelt in the snow to examine the giant footprints dotting their way deeper into the mountains, and the Blue Witch stuck her head in from beside me and recoiled.

“Whoa! Wh-What is this? A human footprint... no, it's way too big.”

“That's why it's Bigfoot. You've never heard of it?”

“What's that? A monster? Not a giant?”

She looked creeped out, so I traced a giant footprint with my finger as I explained.

Bigfoot was an Unidentified Mysterious Animal—a UMA for short—that had caught on during the occult boom at the end of the twentieth century. Nessie, Tsuchinoko, Chupacabra: back in the day, creepy-cute critters like that, the kind that could pass for laid-back local mascots, had been household names.

Bigfoot was a hairy giant that lived deep in the mountains and, true to its name, was famous for leaving huge footprints. Some said it was an ape-man, others a surviving primitive human from a line that had split off from ours. That romantic backstory hit the sweet spot where it seemed like it really could exist, and it had always gotten me going.

But even after I'd filled her in on Bigfoot, the Blue Witch was skeptical.

“So it's a humanoid monster, right? I can't imagine something like this was on Earth before the Gremlin Disaster.”

“No, no, who knows? Maybe monsters had been hiding on Earth all along, and the Gremlin Disaster made them show themselves.”

Kappa, karakasa-obake, stuff like that—who could say that some good old homegrown spooks weren't mixed in among all the monsters running rampant?

I argued with enough heat to melt the snow around us, and the Blue Witch seemed to catch the bug too, leaning in with excitement written all over her face.

“Then nekomata, the nine-tailed fox, and kamaitachi[^2] could be real too...?”

“You really love fluffy creatures, huh. I'd say there's a chance.”

With the suddenly gung-ho Blue Witch in tow, I set off after the footprints of our provisional Bigfoot.

Wooo, we're UMA hunters!

If we can prove Bigfoot exists, there's hope that other UMAs and yokai exist too.

This could be the discovery of a new species with a huge impact on both monster studies and biology!

The giant footprints threaded between the trees, deeper and deeper into the mountains. They were pressed so deep into the snow that there was no losing them.

The Blue Witch took the lead, following the tracks. She looked back, waited for me to catch up, and asked.

“So what'll you do if the thing at the end of these tracks is just a regular old guy with ridiculously huge feet?”

“That'd be fine too. A guy with 40 cm feet walking barefoot through snowy mountains like these? That'd be pretty hilarious.”

“...True.”

I hated meeting people, but someone that weird, I kind of wanted to see at least once.

Finding Bigfoot would be great. Finding a weird old guy would be great too.

Either way, this expedition was a guaranteed win.

The snow was gradually getting heavier, and it felt like a blizzard was on the way. But before the white closed in completely, we found what we were looking for.

The first thing we heard was a high-pitched cry. The farther we went, the louder the cries got, and the more of them there were.

What appeared in front of us as we pushed through the snowy mountains was a troop of Japanese macaques.

They were up in the branches of a huge tree, bare of leaves and capped with snow.

And the hostile troop was shrieking kee-kee over and over, threatening a huge creature at the foot of the tree.

“Hey, Ori. Look over there.”

“I see it. Shh, keep quiet.”

Keeping low and whispering, we watched the commotion, hearts pounding.

The creature the macaques were threatening was Bigfoot, no doubt about it.

It was a humanoid about 3 m tall, huge and shaggy, covered head to toe in gray fur, with a stocky build and especially big feet. A distinctive animal stink stung my nose, and the Blue Witch pinched hers shut.

It was real.

Bigfoot was real!

...But my excitement was over almost as soon as it started.

As we held our breath and watched the mythical creature, I started to piece together what was going on.

The macaques seemed to want to drive Bigfoot off. They shrieked kee-kee and shook the branches, dumping snow on it.

Bigfoot, snow all over its head, scrunched its ugly monkey face into something even uglier, its shoulders slumping.

When it let out a deep, grating growl and gently reached one huge arm up into the tree, a mother monkey clutching her baby screamed and clawed at it.

“Oloron,” Bigfoot howled mournfully. A gray Gremlin glinted on the tip of its nose, telling us what Bigfoot really was.

“Ori. This is...”

“Yeah. It's a monkey that mutated into a monster.”

The owner of the footprints wasn't Bigfoot after all.

It was a monkey that had mutated into a monster. It certainly looked the part of Bigfoot, but it hadn't always looked like that.

It didn't look like its companions anymore, but it still tried to rejoin the troop, only to be turned away.

S-So sad. Could a monster get any sadder than this?

Don't do this to me. I'm the kind of guy who watches some cheap animal documentary and bawls his eyes out over it. Even if it is a hairy, smelly, giant monkey, seeing it that sad makes my chest ache.

“...Maybe I'm one of the lucky ones. I'm not human anymore, but I still get to live in human society.”

“Oooof, heavy. That's got way too much firsthand experience behind it.”

The Blue Witch was hurting too, just in a different way from me.

We'd come looking for a cute, fluffy white wolf, and this was one hell of a surprise. This wasn't the kind of thing I'd wanted to see.

My mood turned gloomy, and as if to rub it in, the sky filled with low, gloomy clouds, and the snow finally started coming down hard.

That was when the sad scene took a turn.

A single white wolf came out of the heavy snow.

It trotted quickly up to Bigfoot and rubbed its muzzle against one thick leg.

Then it stood proudly on all fours beside Bigfoot and barked fiercely at the monkeys, who were shrieking kee-kee with open hostility.

Its furious roar echoed across the snowy mountains.

Faced with a bark louder than their whole troop put together, the monkeys panicked and scattered in an instant.

With the monkeys gone, the white wolf howled in triumph at the sky. Set in its forehead was a blue Gremlin, just like the Blue Witch had said.

Bigfoot, still slumped and dejected, gave the white wolf a single pat as it pressed close in comfort, then trudged away into the white of the heavy snowfall.

The two shadows faded away into the winter.

And so the monsters' little drama on the snowy mountain came to an end: sad, but with a handful of comfort mixed in.

“I see. So monsters live in symbiosis too.”

“Huh?”

I'd summed up the rare sight we'd just witnessed and murmured in admiration, but the Blue Witch sounded like she'd heard some foreign language she didn't know.

What? The Blue Witch is unfamiliar with symbiosis? You were a high school girl, weren't you? Did you even pay attention in biology?

“Symbiosis is when living things of different species live together and affect each other. If both sides benefit, that's mutualism. If only one side benefits, that's commensalism. If one side benefits and makes the other suffer, that's parasitism. Judging by what we just saw, the white wolf was helping Bigfoot, so at the very least it wasn't parasitism, and—”

“Wait, wait, wait. I know what symbiosis is. That's not it. That's not what I mean—why do you try to make things so complicated? Those two were friends. That's all you need to know, right?”

Friends? That mutt and that ape? There's a whole saying about dogs and monkeys not getting along. No way.

What? Am I wrong here?

“Ugh, fine, fine, I get it. I'll listen to your annoying friendship theories as much as you want, so let's get down the mountain already. The blizzard's starting.”

She took me by the hand so we wouldn't get separated as the mountain went white, and we made our way down the trail. It was rare for Okutama to get this much snow.

We'd ended up witnessing some unexpected monster ecology, and I had plenty to say about it, but I'd also gotten to see the white wolf the Blue Witch had told me about.

Well, it wasn't a bad outing.
