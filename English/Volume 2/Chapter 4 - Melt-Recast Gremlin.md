In any era, a first-rate craftsman had to be well versed in materials science.

Carbon nanotubes, the latest synthetic fibers, heat-resistant materials, and so on had shaken up not just industry but the world of hand craftsmen too.

Long ago, blacksmiths set up their workshops near mines and iron-sand deposits to get good iron, and carpenters knew their building materials well enough to pick the right ones for each building.

Craftsmen who understood their materials and used good ones sent the quality of their work soaring.

I was the most dexterous man in the world, but when it came to materials science, I had to admit I was barely more than an amateur.

I prided myself on being the world's one and only Wand Maker, which made me the greatest one too, so from here on I needed to master not just how to process my materials, magic stones and Gremlins, but their properties as well.

I didn't let my guard down. Even after making Cyanos and Aleister, I was nowhere near done honing my skills.

Even back when I made money selling anime goods on online auctions, cheap knockoffs of my work (apparently someone had put a 3D printer to good use) had gone around and eaten into my market share.

At the moment, no Wand Maker was better than me.

But if I rested on my laurels, latecomers would copy my techniques, overtake me, and sneer, “That guy used to be amazing, but look at him now, lol.”

Sure, my dexterity wasn't something people could easily copy, but if some breakthrough brought electricity back and all that dusty precision machinery started running again, it would be a whole different story.

Never, ever let your guard down.

One autumn day, a year after the Dragon Witch kidnapped and imprisoned me, I finished the reverberatory furnace on the mountain behind my house and began a Gremlin-melting experiment.

Gremlins, the core of every magic wand, were an important general-purpose material and easy to come by. If I was going to learn more, this was the place to start.

Being able to judge a Gremlin's quality by magic-excitation acoustic appraisal wasn't nearly enough. I wanted my knowledge to go much wider and much deeper.

I lit the reverberatory furnace and pumped the bellows with my foot to feed it air, rereading my notes to go back over the plan for this experiment.

It was well known that the bigger a Gremlin or magic stone was, the stronger its magic-amplification effect. But even the biggest natural Gremlins were only 40 mm across, so if I wanted a bigger mass, I'd have to make one artificially.

Shaving several Gremlins to fit together, or joining them with adhesive, didn't make them count as a single mass. So my thinking was simple: if I melted a whole bunch of Gremlins at high heat and let them cool and harden, wouldn't I get one giant mass?

Gremlins were milky-white crystals that resembled quartz, so it looked like they'd melt the same way quartz did.

Of course, anybody could come up with an idea like that. The Flame Witch had tried it ages ago.

The Blue Witch had apparently been part of the Gremlin melt-synthesis experiment run soon after the Gremlin Disaster, so she told me how it had turned out.

The Flame Witch's experiment had been a failure. Basic, low-firepower fire magic didn't melt the Gremlins at all, and when she used her highest-firepower fire magic, they didn't melt either. They gave off violet lightning and vanished into dust.

That result led people to guess that Gremlins, which grew by absorbing electricity, held on to the electricity they absorbed, and energy experiments to extract usable electricity from Gremlins got underway. But after Minato Ward's experimental facilities and research materials burned and its researchers scattered, that work had stalled, and it still hadn't restarted.

A pretty interesting result, but a failure was a failure.

Then again, they said failure was the mother of success.

The destruction of Minato Ward had put the Gremlin experiments on hold indefinitely, but that same destruction had also brought a new fact to light.

Minato Ward had been destroyed last spring, when the giant kaiju came ashore.

Fires broke out in the buildings the giant kaiju wrecked, and with nobody to fight them, they spread until Minato Ward burned to ash.

Then, one after another, Gremlins that had melted, cooled, and hardened again turned up in the scorched wasteland Minato Ward had become.

Gremlins were supposed to give off violet lightning and turn to dust in high heat, yet for some reason the ones in the Minato Ward ruins had melted once and then hardened.

I figured the reason lay in the difference between magical fire and natural fire.

Take the ice from the Blue Witch's Great Glacier magic, for example. Ordinary fire couldn't melt it; only magical flames could.

In fact, a year and a half after the kaiju died, the work to thaw Hamura City, which Great Glacier magic had frozen from end to end, was still only about 50% done. The ice had defied every attempt to melt it with anything but magical fire.

In other words, where magic was concerned, magical fire and natural fire were two different things that behaved differently.

Naturally, the Flame Witch had used magical fire for her Gremlin-melting experiment.

But the great fire in Minato Ward had been natural fire.

The fierce heat of that huge natural blaze had melted the Gremlins, and once the fire burned itself out, they cooled and hardened.

This hypothesis seemed worth testing.

First, I tried melting Gremlins in a campfire.

Which, well, failed, just like I'd known it would.

A campfire topped out at around 800°C. That could melt lead, but not copper, and not glass. Not Gremlins either.

If a natural fire as weak as a campfire could melt Gremlins, Gremlin melt-synthesis would have caught on ages ago.

In this post-apocalyptic world, where the electricity was gone and survivors had long since used up the gas and gasoline, getting a fire any hotter than a campfire took real effort.

But this experiment was worth the effort. It would've been one thing if the outcome were a toss-up, but this one had good odds of success.

According to data I'd dug up after breaking into a fire station, a major fire could reach as high as 1,200°C.

Assuming Gremlins melted at 1,200°C, a climbing kiln, the kind used for pottery and other crafts, could hit that temperature. Building one would be a pain, but not insanely hard.

But as long as I was at it, I wanted enough heat to melt iron too. I'd used up every decent iron plate I had reinforcing the walls of the workshop the Dragon Witch smashed, so a furnace that could melt iron would come in handy for making iron goods down the line.

So I built a reverberatory furnace that could hit 1,600°C, hot enough for iron.

A reverberatory furnace was a sophisticated piece of work, capable of some of the highest temperatures you could get without electricity or gas.

Getting enough bricks to build it had been a struggle.

The Blue Witch had helped me with a lot more than making bricks, too. For one thing, she'd cast Lost Mist over the whole Okutama area for me.

Lost Mist spread fog over a wide area and made every intruder except the caster lose their sense of direction, but with the kind of magic-power control only a witch could manage, she'd left me out of its targets.

She was even considerate enough to keep the fog off the rice paddies and fields so they still got sunlight. Seriously, that was a huge help. Even if I cast Lost Mist myself, I couldn't pull off anything that delicate.

To thank her, I gave her a rare card that had once sold for as much as 16,000 yen at a card shop, but she didn't seem all that happy about it.

Anyway, with the Blue Witch's help, I built the reverberatory furnace on the mountain behind my house, working from old reverberatory-furnace blueprints and the fanbook of a TV variety group that had built one of their own in modern times. But partway through, a problem came up.

It turned out that a Gremlin that had been melted once and hardened again lost its function as a magic-activation medium.

I found this out when Professor Ohinata heard about my experiment from the Blue Witch—who got stupidly loose-lipped, but only with Ome residents—and sent me a sample of melt-recast Gremlin collected in Minato Ward.

No wonder the Gremlin-melting experiments had been frozen. Losing the research materials was part of it, but mostly it was because the results had come out as “Looks like it's kind of a bust.”

Melt a bunch of small Gremlins down and set them into one giant Gremlin, and all you got was a milky-white crystal. You could cast a spell right next to it and it wouldn't make a peep. It was nothing but a rock.

Finding out the experiment was a failure before I'd even run it got me down, but I bounced back by setting myself a different goal.

The Gremlin melt-dyeing experiment.

Gremlins were basically crystals that grew by absorbing electricity, but monsters had Gremlins too.

And unlike the milky-white ones that grew on electricity, monster Gremlins came in colors.

For example, the Gremlin I got from a rabbit monster way back was red. The white wolf monster the Blue Witch was so fond of had a blue one, and Bigfoot's was gray.

Colored Gremlins performed no differently from milky-white ones, but just having color made them feel special somehow, and I really loved that.

Melting and recasting Gremlins would help me get at the secret behind those colored ones.

Could I color them by adding a metal compound, the way you could with glass?

Or would it take a nonmetal compound?

What color would I get if I mixed a red Gremlin with a milky-white one?

And so on. There was a lot I could do with melting experiments in the reverberatory furnace.

Not that knowing all about dyeing Gremlins had any real benefit, but that was only true for now.

Research that seems useless today might turn out to be useful as hell someday, somewhere. Or it might never be useful for anything, ever. That's basic research, and that's the kind of materials science a craftsman needs.

Besides, a craftsman sitting on a mountain of useless but fun knowledge gives off a serious pro vibe. It's cool.

Professor Ohinata's funding came from the Foresight Mage, and by extension the Tokyo Witches' Council, with strings attached, so she could only do research that was “useful right away.” Not me. I was free to research whatever I wanted, as much as I wanted.

Going solo came with plenty of downsides and inconveniences, but at times like this, I was glad I liked being alone.

I finished rereading every bit of research history and analysis in my notebook, and the reverberatory furnace still wasn't anywhere near up to temperature.

At this rate, I'd have to keep pumping the bellows for another two hours.

Magical fire would have heated it up in no time. But the nature of the experiment meant I had to grind the temperature up with natural fire, and that was rough.

I wiped away sweat and kept pumping the bellows. This is harder than I thought. I'm gonna wreck my back.

Two hours of dull, heavy labor later, I was completely beat, but I finally confirmed that the Gremlins packed into the crucible inside the furnace had melted into goo. Then I started slowly bringing the furnace temperature down.

Gremlins seemed crystalline, so if I dropped the temperature too fast, they'd probably crack as they hardened. The plan was to ease the fire down little by little, seal the furnace vents with dirt once it went out, and then wait a full day for the temperature to come down on its own.

It took three hours of banking the fire before it finally went out. After six hours of fire work in total, I was wiped out and dragged myself back home for a bath. Between the heat, the sweat, and the soot, everything smelled of smoke and my throat was parched. Just miserable.

Tossing charcoal into the furnace to keep it fueled had been surprisingly hard work too.

Still, it had been a good sweat. If I did this regularly, I'd probably put on some muscle.

The reverberatory furnace had taken me a solid six months to build, starting from the design stage, and it ran smoothly without a single hitch, handing me a huge amount of experimental data.

Bottom line: the coloring of Gremlins seemed to have something to do with components in monster blood.

A three-legged crow the Blue Witch hunted in Okutama had a light-green Gremlin at the base of its beak. When I boiled down and dried that crow's blood and mixed what was left into melted Gremlins, they turned light green.

Doing the same with ordinary crow blood didn't produce any color, so it seemed certain that some blood component unique to monsters was what colored the Gremlins (powdered bone or skin didn't color them either).

While I was at it, the coloring experiments also taught me something that was nothing but downside: wrap an ordinary Gremlin in melt-recast Gremlin, cast magic through it, and the magic-power consumption doubled.

Seriously useless, but if I built a prison or something out of melt-recast Gremlin, it might keep prisoners from using magic. Maybe. Probably.

Then again, it only doubled magic-power consumption, so anyone willing to spend twice the magic power could still cast normally. As a way to stop magic, it wasn't all that reliable.

Well, this experimental data would come in handy someday too. Probably.

Besides, even if the principle behind coloring Gremlins was no use for my main job of making magic wands, it did help with my side job making accessories. Looked at that way, it wasn't bad at all.

Now then.

While I was building a reverberatory furnace and goofing around with hobby research and processing that did nothing whatsoever to rebuild Japan's collapsed society, reconstruction in the cities kept moving forward, led by the Tokyo Witches' Council, with everyone sometimes pulling together and sometimes not.

Of all the reconstruction news that reached me through Professor Ohinata's letters, what freaked me out most was that trains were running again.

Not sleek steel boxes, mind you. They were freight trains made of low-horsepower charcoal-powered vehicles coupled together, and they only ran along roughly 25 km of line repurposed from the former Tokyo Metro Marunouchi Line.

Even so, the return of public transportation gave everyone a huge ray of hope. Professor Ohinata had attended the opening ceremony, and apparently the crowd was half people in tears and half people cheering.

It really was amazing. Sure, it was possible in theory, but actually pulling it off so fast, with supplies and labor so scarce, was seriously impressive.

Getting freight trains running again only two and a half years after the Gremlin Disaster was Japan living up to its name as a technological powerhouse.

It convinced me that, just as Tokyo had risen from the ashes after World War II, the city would rise from the Gremlin Disaster like a phoenix too.

The next biggest reconstruction story after the trains had to be the spread of fertility magic, which was still being pushed hard, just like last year.

The fertility-magic bypass incantation had spread fast over the past year.

Since learning it was a matter of life or death, more than 95% of Tokyo residents aged 12 and over had picked it up.

For children 11 and under, the policy was apparently not to teach it, as a rule. People worried about kids casting fertility magic on crops the wrong way out of curiosity, or passing out when they ran out of magic power and hitting their heads or falling (Tokyo Magic University entrants were the exception and could learn it even at 11 or younger).

This year's food production had reached 98% of what was needed, and projections said that next year, with farmland expanding, the food self-sufficiency rate would hit 102%.

That was an unbelievably fast turnaround, considering that until last year people had been screaming, “Impossible. We're headed for starvation hell.”

But when I thought about it calmly, it was nuts that even with a bugged spell that could instantly more than double crop yields, the self-sufficiency rate was still only around 100%. Without magic, simple math put it at 50%. No wonder things had turned into a hellscape.

Even I, enjoying the carefree bachelor life out in the country, had been helped a ton when the yields from my fields and rice paddies doubled. The people in the cities who'd been rationing food must have cried so many happy tears they'd dried themselves out.

As a tech guy myself, I knew full well that behind that flashy reconstruction story lay brutal effort and impossible demands.

I was making and shipping general-purpose, mass-produced magic wands for Tokyo Magic University, which had started trialing a one-year curriculum, and in exchange I got first dibs on sugar, soy sauce, miso, seafood, clothes, and other supplies and luxuries I'd have trouble getting on my own.

But Professor Ohinata had it way harder than I did. On top of teaching classes as a professor, she was throwing herself into research and recruiting people every day to set up a new department, all to make Tokyo Magic University the center of magic research. Apparently the magic wands made great bait for recruiting, too.

Nobody knew where the wands came from, but among Witches' Council members and the experts involved in government, the leading theory seemed to be that a Wand Maker was being hidden in Ome.

The Blue Witch owned the overtechnology Blue Wand Cyanos, with nuclear-bomb-class amplification, and she'd made Ome her base and set up an ironclad defense there. Professor Ohinata owned the dodecahedral fractal wand Aleister, a freakishly processed piece of work that looked like no human hands could have made it, and she was from Ome too, with close ties to the Blue Witch.

On top of that, rumor had it that the Dragon Witch had kidnapped someone from Ome and gotten a leg torn off by the Blue Witch.

You'd have to be an idiot not to suspect Ome after all that.

Except the Wand Maker in question was actually me, living the easy craftsman life in Okutama, right next door to Ome. No way they'd ever figure that out.

Thanks to the Blue Witch and Professor Ohinata protecting my secret and my safety (and to the Dragon Witch, whom the Blue Witch had threatened into keeping my secret), I got to live alone in this collapsed world just like I wanted. But I did have one complaint: wand making had been getting kind of stale lately.

For the past year, all I'd done was obsess over wand details, with no progress on better performance or new features. I ran useless experiments here and there, melt-recast Gremlin chief among them, but no good ideas for pushing wands forward came to me. I was in a slump of sorts.

At times like this, nothing beat hearing from the customers.

So I wrote to Professor Ohinata, a heavy user of magic wands, asking, “Got any requests? A magic wand you wish existed?” A reply came back at breakneck speed, a letter laying out a very detailed request for a backlash-prevention magic wand.

The request was more specific than I'd imagined, and it piqued my interest.

Apparently, out on the front lines of magic research and application, my magic wands still came up short in plenty of ways.

Now we're talking. This, this is what I was after. A craftsman's whole job is to come through when a customer says, “I want this!”

All right! The next wand I'll make is a backlash-prevention magic wand!
